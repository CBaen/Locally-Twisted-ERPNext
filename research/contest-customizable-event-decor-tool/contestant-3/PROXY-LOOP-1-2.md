# Proxy Loop 1-2 — Contestant 3 (The Coloring Page Frame)
## Jeff's-Perspective Probe

Your design card is the most complete handoff artifact in the field. You've included: the visual composition, color name labels, AND hex codes — "Seafoam #88FED0," "Blush #F4DFD7," "Lime Pastel #B8FF9E" — all on the card. That hex-code list is the thing Jeff takes to his balloon supplier. You've closed the loop from customer's intention all the way to Jeff's sourcing conversation, and you've done it inside a single screen that the customer experiences as a satisfying moment of capture. That's the brief's handoff vision made concrete. The phone number in the footer ("Or call: (801) 285-0860") is a quiet detail that tells a customer who doesn't want to wait for a form response that there's another path — and it tells Jeff that the tool respects his existing contact preference.

Now the probe from Jeff's side.

---

**The screenshot question — is it the handoff or a supplement?**

Your REASONING Q4 says: "The card is designed to be screenshotted and texted to a friend or spouse — the sharing mechanic doesn't require us to build sharing." That's correct about sharing. But it introduces a question about the Jeff-specific path: if the primary sharing mechanic is screenshot-and-text, does Jeff receive a screenshot? Or does he receive a separate form submission?

Looking at your 05-done screen: "Send this to Jeff →" is a button inside the card, not beneath it. That CTA is inside the card's `design-card-footer`. If the customer screenshots the card and texts it, Jeff gets an image. If the customer taps "Send this to Jeff," Jeff gets a form submission. These are two different handoff artifacts with different qualities. The screenshot gives Jeff the visual but no structured data he can log in a CRM. The form submission gives him structured data (piece names, hex codes) but loses the visual unless you attach it.

Articulate in your reasoning which path is primary, and what the form submission specifically contains. The hex codes are on the card — they should be in the form payload too. If both paths land on Jeff simultaneously (the form fires AND includes the card visual as a summary), that's the ideal outcome and worth describing.

---

**The Zeigarnik empty slots and what Jeff reads**

Your composition view (04) shows two pieces colored (arch, column) plus two empty slots labeled "Add a centerpiece?" and "Add a garland?" with silhouetted shapes. From Jeff's perspective: if the customer sends a 2-piece inquiry after seeing those slots, Jeff knows the tool offered them more and they declined. That's useful information — it tells Jeff the customer may be budget-conscious or may genuinely want two pieces. But it also means Jeff may feel he needs to re-offer what the tool already surfaced, which is redundant if the customer already said no by not adding the piece.

Is that a problem? Maybe not. The empty slots don't create pressure — they're quiet silhouettes, not aggressive "ADD THIS" prompts. But Jeff's pitch "I also have columns and centerpieces if you're interested" might feel like he's repeating what they already saw. Consider whether your reasoning should acknowledge this: the tool already surfaces adjacent options, so Jeff's pitch should start from "tell me about the event" not "would you like a column too." The tool does the soft upsell; Jeff does the context-gathering. That's a better division of labor and your design naturally produces it.

---

**Illustration fidelity and Jeff's pitch**

Your arch SVG in the done-card is a small 60×50 viewBox — 7 circle balloons in a symmetric arc. At the size it renders in the card (around 60px wide rendered), this is identifiable as an arch but barely. Jeff sees the shape name "Arch" plus the color swatches, so he knows what was ordered. But does the illustration add anything to his pitch preparation, or is the text summary (piece names + hex codes) doing all the real work?

This is worth being honest about in your reasoning: the card visual earns its place emotionally (the customer sees their thing in miniature, it feels like a memento) but Jeff's pitch preparation probably runs off the text summary, not the illustration. That's fine — the illustration serves the customer moment, the text serves Jeff. Both belong on the card. Just be clear which is doing which job.

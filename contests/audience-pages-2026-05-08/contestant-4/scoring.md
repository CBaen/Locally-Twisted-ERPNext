## Contestant 1 — Audience Authority

- **Audience fit: 9/10.** The operational specificity per buyer is the strongest in the field.
  The civic procurement section (W-9, COI, PO/net-30, multi-venue) is not marketing copy — it

  claim, KJSCOTT in structural position, weight-commensurate container. Every page was built
  from inside the buyer's job, not from outside it.

- **Proof density: 9/10.** The corporate scrolling crawl displays all 30 clients in motion —
  the full roster as infrastructure rather than a list. The civic grid names 26 organizations
  with category labels. The private page deploys six real Google reviews including the KJSCOTT
  memorial review and the seven-year relationship review. The only reason this isn't a 10 is
  that the schools page leans on three named clients appropriately, but the brief's roster is
  genuinely thin there — that's a brief constraint, not a design failure. No invented clients.
  No inflated stats.

- **Style discipline: 8/10.** The visual differentiation across the suite (Slate Blue civic,
  Ink/crawl corporate, Slate overlay schools, Warm private) is deliberate and executed. Hero
  contract honored. Container discipline documented in DESIGN-NOTES. The one reservation:
  the brass bottom-border reversal on schools is a subtle differentiator that's easy to
  implement correctly and easy to silently regress. The intent is documented but it's a
  fragile signal compared to C2's border-direction logic, which is explicitly theorized in
  the README with a posture vocabulary a future builder can consult. Not a violation — a
  risk register observation.

- **Total: 26/30**
- **Notes:** The Loop 2 maintainer-protection note in civic DESIGN-NOTES ("DO NOT PARAPHRASE
  OR SOFTEN the four civic buyer notes cards") is the most operationally disciplined
  documentation in the field. That's a real asset for implementation.

## Self-Reflection — Contestant 4 ("One System, Four Buyers")

Reading all seven peers honestly, and then reading my own work with the same frame:

**Where my suite earned its place:** The brand-color swatch section on corporate (named client
palettes — KSL navy, Zions red, Chick-fil-A) is the only visual proof-of-color-matching in
the field. It demonstrates the capability rather than claiming it. The schools editorial ledger
(brass rule separators, specific event context per school) was an accepted proxy correction that
improved proof density. The private memorial architecture (proof before the grid in deep navy,
invitation after the grid in warm white — two structural rooms for two buyer states) was held
against the proxy's Jeff-test note with documented reasoning.

**Where my suite is weaker than top peers:** Proof density on civic is lower than C5, C6, or C7.
My civic page has a category-grouped roster (post Round 2 loop correction) but fewer named-client
narrative moments and case stories than C6 or C7. My corporate page has the brand-color swatch
section as a differentiator but the broader proof register is less dense than C5's AP/billing
standalone section or C7's sector-grouped narrative. The memorial architecture on private
(two sections, two registers) is documented as intentional but carries the risk the proxy
identified — a first-time reader may experience it as repetition rather than contrast if the
visual register difference (navy/brass vs. warm-white/stone) doesn't read clearly on the
actual rendered page. That's an implementation risk, not just a design risk.

Honest position: I'm at the 25/30 tier — behind C1, C2, C5, C7 in the scoring I assigned
them, and comparable to C6. The brand-color swatch section is a genuine structural
differentiator. The proof density on the civic and corporate pages is the main gap relative
to the top scorers.

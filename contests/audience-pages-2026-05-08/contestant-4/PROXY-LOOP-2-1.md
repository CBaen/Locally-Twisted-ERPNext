# Proxy Loop 2-1 — Contestant 4 (One System, Four Buyers)

## Lens: The Jeff-test

Imagine GL opens your suite and shows it to Jeff. Not the code — the pages,
rendered, in a browser. Where does Jeff smile? Where does Jeff get stuck?

Jeff will smile at the corporate brand-color swatches section immediately. He's a
visual person; seeing Chick-Fil-A red and KSL navy presented as matching swatches
says "this person understands my actual work" in a way that a paragraph of copy
cannot. That section was the right call for exactly that reason.

Where Jeff might get stuck: the two memorial sections on private celebrations.
Section 2 (the pre-grid KJSCOTT callout) and Section 4 (the standalone
"Celebrations of Life" section) serve two different structural purposes — the first
catches the grief buyer early, the second gives the grief buyer a full home. Both
are intentional. But to Jeff, who isn't reading with an architectural lens, they
might read as repetition: "why are we talking about memorials twice?"

The answer is in the architecture, not on the page. And Jeff won't be reading the
architecture. He'll be reading the page. So the question is: does the page's
sequence make the repetition feel cumulative rather than circular? Does the second
memorial section add something the first didn't have — a different register, a
different CTA, a different invitation?

Right now the first section is proof-centered (KJSCOTT's review) and the second is
invitation-centered ("If you're here for a celebration of life, you're in the right
place" + two invitation paragraphs). Those ARE different things. The risk is that
they feel similar if the two sections don't have enough visual contrast between them
to signal "this is a different kind of moment."

If Jeff says "you mention memorials twice" — the answer is "yes, and here's why
it's not the same thing twice." That answer should be legible from the page itself,
not just from the DESIGN-NOTES.

What's working: the four-room furniture test is the most clearly documented
concept in the field. Every room has something that can't be transplanted. That's
the discipline that wins against generic suites.

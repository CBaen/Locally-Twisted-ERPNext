# Proxy Loop 2-2 — Contestant 2 (The Right Room)

## Lens: The buyer's recall test

Two weeks after a Utah city events coordinator reads your civic page, what's the
one thing she remembers? This test matters because purchase decisions — especially
civic ones with committee approval layers — often happen days or weeks after the
first reading.

Right now, your civic page's strongest structural move is the four-category client
proof grid (Cities & Counties, Pride & Equality, Chambers, Community Venues). It
answers "have they done work like mine?" immediately and clearly. But the memorable
moment — the thing she tells her colleague about — isn't in the grid. The grid is
evidence. Evidence doesn't travel; stories do.

Compare this to C7's civic page, which has one sentence that a coordinator might
actually repeat aloud: "When the city puts it on a banner, the balloons have to
hold up." That sentence is quotable precisely because it names the stakes of civic
balloon work in a way no one has said quite that way before. Your civic page has
strong proof density but the one repeatable line hasn't arrived yet.

The opportunity is in your case stories section — three installations (SLC Pride,
Gallivan Center, Ogden City). Each of those has a story moment inside it. SLC Pride
is photographed by press. Gallivan Center is the city's public living room. Ogden
City works with municipal procurement. One of those three has a line like C7's
waiting to be written. The question is which installation has the highest-stakes
version of "and then the city photographed it."

What's working: the procurement cards section you added in Round 2 ("screenshot
for supervisor") is the sharpest single observation about civic buyer behavior in
the entire field. That instruction — showing a civic planner exactly what to do
with the information — is worth more than a capability list. It treats the buyer
as someone who has organizational politics to navigate, not just a decision to make.

# Proxy Loop 2-1 — Contestant 7 (The Proof is in the Place)

## Lens: The editorial-publication editor

If your suite went to a design publication, here's what would get spotlighted:
"When the city puts it on a banner, the balloons have to hold up." It's the most
quotable single sentence in the field — identified by the Proxy in Round 1, still
the field's high-water mark for civic copy compression. A publication would pull
that sentence into a sidebar. They'd probably also spotlight "Private by Default"
as a structural naming move, because no other contestant gave privacy its own
structural commitment.

Here's what the publication editor would cut: the schools service note section.
Read it against the schools proof trio (graduation, back-to-school, campus events).
The proof trio is specific — Weber State purple named, graduation timing named,
venue windows named. The service note below it describes "Color-Matched School
Spirit" and "Schedule Disciplined" at a level of generality that the proof section
already exceeded. The service note is repeating what the proof already showed, less
vividly.

This is the compression question that editorial editors push hardest: if the proof
section already shows it, does the service note need to say it? Sometimes the answer
is yes — the buyer needs the explicit statement as a purchase-decision anchor, not
just the implicit proof. But if the service note is just a softer version of the
proof, it costs attention without adding evidence.

Your schools page's editorial ledger (three schools, brass-rule separators) is the
strongest version of a short-roster-as-feature in the field. The proof section
earns it. The question is whether the service note is adding its own weight to what
the ledger already built, or echoing it.

What's working: the civic roster reorganization from flat tags to four-column grouped
grid was exactly the right Round 2 move. A county fair coordinator finding "Cities &
Counties" without scanning Pride organizations first is a UX improvement that proves
you're thinking about how the buyer's eye moves, not just what the page contains.

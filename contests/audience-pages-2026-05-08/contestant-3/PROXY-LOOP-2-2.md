# Proxy Loop 2-2 — Contestant 3 (Made For You / Anxiety-First)

## Lens: The proof-vs-promise integrity test

C3 is the most explicitly theorized suite in the field — the README articulates
the persuasion architecture as a testable claim ("a reviewer should be able to
point to the anxiety-naming move on every page without being told where to look").
That's a high bar. Let's check whether the proof holds it up.

The corporate page makes a strong promise through the H1 ("On-brand. On-time.
Invoice-ready."). Those three promises need a proof section for each one. "On-brand"
is proven by the named client roster and any color-matching claims. "On-time" is
promised but where is it proven? The client roster doesn't prove timeliness. A
review excerpt that mentions on-time delivery would close that gap — and there are
verified reviews that speak to this ("delivered on time" appears in the KJSCOTT
review itself, which is currently on private, not corporate).

The point isn't to move KJSCOTT to corporate — that would be wrong. The point is:
the H1 is making a three-part promise, and the page's proof structure should
consciously answer all three parts. Right now it may be answering "on-brand" and
"invoice-ready" more completely than "on-time."

This is the proof-vs-promise integrity question: when the page makes a named promise,
does the proof structure systematically answer it, or does the promise outrun the
evidence? For C3, where the architecture is explicitly documented as a testable
claim, this matters more than for any other suite. The architecture promises that
every page has an answerable structure. The corporate page's three-part H1 sets up
a three-part answer that might be worth auditing.

What's working: the private page is now fully executing the anxiety-first
architecture on the hardest audience — the grief buyer. "When a celebration carries
grief, the decor has to hold both" names the exact tension the buyer is feeling
before the page asks her for anything. That's the architecture working at its best.

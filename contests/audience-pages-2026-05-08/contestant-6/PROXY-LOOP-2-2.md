# Proxy Loop 2-2 — Contestant 6 (Buyer-Scoped Authority)

## Lens: The buyer's recall test (civic edition)

Your suite holds its structural architecture cleanly across all four pages. The
shared grammar (hero → dark stats → photo → named proof → service → practical
note → CTA) is consistent enough that a reader moving from one page to another
feels the same organizational intelligence at work. That's a real differentiator
for a suite that's trying to communicate "one company, four audiences."

Now apply the two-weeks-later recall test to your civic page specifically. A Sandy
City events coordinator reads it, files it away, brings it to her planning meeting.
What does she say about the page?

She'll remember the proof density — 60+ civic events, the grid of named
organizations. She'll remember the PO/billing capability ("experienced with
municipal procurement") because that's the practical thing she needed confirmed.
What she may not remember is anything about the QUALITY of the civic work — not
the capability, but the character of what it felt like when LT showed up at a city
event.

Your stats section claims "Since 1998" — that's twenty-seven years of civic events.
That number, if it's accurate, is an extraordinary signal. Does the civic page make
anything of it? Or does it sit in the stats bar alongside "60+ civic events" as
one of four proof numbers?

"Twenty-seven years of public events in Utah" contains something specific: LT was
at civic events before the coordinator's current director was in the role. That's
institutional continuity of a kind most vendors can't claim. The stats band has
that number; a sentence in the case study or proof intro could make it land.

What's working: your civic page's "Parade to Podium" stat framing is the best
scope-signal shorthand in any civic section — it names the full range of civic
contexts in three words. That's a copywriting move that earns its place in the
stats bar.
